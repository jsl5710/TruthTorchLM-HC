\title{CoI-Verbalized: Discriminative Self-Consistency Verification \\
Beats Verbalized Confidence on Long-Form Factuality}

\author{Anonymous \\ \texttt{} \\}

\begin{abstract}
Verbalized confidence---asking a language model to rate how likely its own answer is
correct---is the default black-box uncertainty signal, but it is overconfident and, on
long-form answers, poorly calibrated. We study \textbf{CoI-Verbalized}, a family of
pure black-box confidence estimators that judge a \emph{fixed} candidate answer by
decomposing it into atomic claims and scoring each claim, then aggregating in code. An
ablation ladder isolates where the signal lives: the self-rated per-claim \emph{risk} used
by a deterministic aggregation ($\phi$) is the weak link, and replacing it with a
\emph{discriminative} verification step---a fresh yes/no ``Is this claim correct?'' asked
once (decision-framed) or $K$ times (self-consistency)---is what matters. Evaluating across
\textbf{six targets spanning 8B open models to 32B/70B open models to frontier closed models
(GPT-4o, Claude-Haiku), over up to twelve datasets}, we find the verification advantage is
\emph{conditional and mechanistic}, not universal: it helps precisely where the verbalized
baseline is weak or \emph{inverted}. On overconfident closed targets, verbalized confidence
falls \emph{below chance} on health long-form (GPT-4o AUROC $0.34$), and self-consistency
verification recovers it to $0.68$ ($+0.34$); on long-form general knowledge it lifts AUROC
$+0.09$ to $+0.11$ over verbalized. But where the baseline is already strong---short factual
QA, MCQ, and \emph{every dataset on the strong open 32B/70B targets} (verification wins
$0/9$)---it adds nothing. Crucially, a strong \emph{open} judge (Llama-70B) still loses health
long-form to verbalized ($0.64$ vs.\ $0.55$): the win is driven by closed-model
\emph{overconfidence}, not judge capability. Against established multi-sample baselines
(semantic entropy, spectral, lexical), CoI-Verbalized is a competitive \emph{single-answer}
estimator that never resamples the target. We release the method and evaluation harness.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Deployed systems increasingly consume answers from language models they cannot inspect:
no logits, no hidden states, no weights, only text. The cheapest confidence signal in
this setting is \emph{verbalized confidence}~\citep{tian2023just,kadavath2022language}:
prompt the model to rate, on a $0$--$100$ scale, how likely its answer is correct. It is
one extra call and needs no training, but it is systematically overconfident, and on
long-form answers---where correctness is a property of many interacting facts---a single
global self-rating is a coarse summary of a structured object.

A natural fix is to make the structure explicit. \textbf{Chain-of-Interaction (CoI)}
verbalized confidence judges a fixed candidate answer $y$ for a query $x$ by (i)
decomposing $y$ into atomic, independently checkable claims, (ii) gathering evidence and
an adversarial critique per claim, and (iii) scanning for cross-claim contradictions;
the confidence \emph{score} is then computed deterministically in code (a fixed-prior
aggregation $\phi$) rather than self-rated, which removes the same-pass overconfidence of
plain verbalized confidence. This is attractive in principle, but raises an empirical
question we answer here: \emph{which part of the chain actually carries the signal?}

We study an ablation ladder (\S\ref{sec:method}) over a six-target grid and report four
findings (\S\ref{sec:results}).
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item The \textbf{self-rated per-claim risk is the weak link}. The deterministic-$\phi$
CoI beats verbalized confidence on some short QA but \emph{collapses below it on long-form},
where each answer contributes many hedged risk scores. Replacing risk with a
\textbf{discriminative yes/no verification} (once, or $K$ times as self-consistency) is
what recovers signal.
\item \textbf{The verification advantage is a minority, not a majority.} Across the full
suite it beats the best baseline on only $2$--$3$ of $11$ datasets on 8B targets and
\textbf{$0$ of $9$ on the strong open 32B/70B targets}. Blanket ``decomposition beats
verbalized confidence'' is false.
\item \textbf{It helps where the baseline is weak or inverted.} On overconfident
\emph{closed} targets, verbalized confidence drops below chance and verification recovers
it---its largest win is health long-form on GPT-4o ($0.34\rightarrow0.68$); closed win-rate
is $4/6$ (GPT-4o) and $3/5$ (Claude). It also wins long-form general knowledge (where $\phi$
collapses) on 8B.
\item \textbf{The mechanism is overconfidence, not judge strength.} A strong \emph{open}
judge (Llama-70B) still loses health long-form to verbalized ($0.64$ vs.\ $0.55$), whereas
weaker but \emph{overconfident} closed judges are where verification wins. Judge capability
does not predict the advantage; baseline inversion does.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Method: CoI-Verbalized}\label{sec:method}
All estimators take a query $x$ and a \emph{fixed} candidate answer $y$ (never rewriting
$y$) and return a confidence in $[0,1]$; higher means more likely correct. They are
\emph{pure black-box}: they read only text, and the judge model is the target itself. We
organize them as a ladder of ``interaction chains'' $n$.

\paragraph{$n_1$: Verbalized confidence (floor).} One call: ``Judge how likely $y$ is
correct; return \{confidence: 0--100\}.'' The standard baseline.

\paragraph{$n_3$: Deterministic $\phi$ (CoI default).} Decompose $y$ into claims; per
claim, the model returns a \emph{grounding} label (contextual/parametric/inferred), an
evidence sentence, a counter-argument, and a self-rated \emph{risk} $r\in[0,1]$; a
consistency chain lists contradictions. Confidence is computed in code:
each claim scores $s = \mathrm{base}[\text{grounding}]\cdot(1-r)\cdot\text{penalty}$,
with a fixed prior $\mathrm{base}=\{1.0,0.85,0.65\}$ and a contradiction penalty; claims
are pooled by a min/mean rule ($\phi$). No training data is used.

\paragraph{$n_6$: Decision-framed verification.} Decompose $y$ into claims; then, instead
of a self-rated risk, issue a \emph{fresh} discriminative call per claim: ``Is the
statement factually correct and a correct answer to the question? Answer YES or NO.''
The per-claim confidence is $1$ for YES and $0$ for NO; claims are pooled by the same
min/mean rule. One verification call per claim (greedy).

\paragraph{$n_7$: Self-consistency verification.} As $n_6$, but each claim is verified
$K{=}5$ times at temperature $0.7$; the per-claim confidence is the \emph{YES-vote
fraction}. The intuition is that hallucinated facts are unstable across samples while
known facts are stable, so vote entropy discriminates where a single self-rating cannot.

The ladder isolates two design choices: $n_1{\to}n_3$ swaps a global self-rating for a
structured self-rating; $n_3{\to}n_6$ swaps self-rated \emph{risk} for a discriminative
\emph{decision}; $n_6{\to}n_7$ adds sampling-based consistency. All chains run with model
``thinking'' disabled, so latency reflects the estimator, not incidental reasoning.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}
\paragraph{Targets.} Six models spanning the capability and access spectrum, each used as
both generator and its own chain-judge (pure black-box): two \textbf{8B open}
(Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen3-8B), two \textbf{large open} (Qwen3-32B, Llama-3.3-70B,
4-bit), and two \textbf{frontier closed} accessed via a gateway (GPT-4o, Claude-Haiku-4.5).
The spread lets us separate judge \emph{capability} from judge \emph{calibration}.

\paragraph{Datasets.} Up to twelve, spanning format (short-factual, long-form, MCQ,
multi-hop, adversarial) and domain (general, health, math): TriviaQA, NaturalQA, PopQA
(general short), \textbf{LongFact}~\citep{wei2024longfact} (general long-form, 300 prompts),
TruthfulQA (adversarial), MedQA/MMLU-Med (MCQ), BioASQ/K-QA (health free), MedLFQA (health
long-form), GSM8K (math), HotpotQA (multi-hop). Each is 150 items unless noted; closed and
large-open targets are evaluated on the subset with cached generations (six datasets each).
Correctness is a gold-grounded LLM judge (Qwen3-4B; letter-match for MCQ); LongFact has no
gold, so we label factuality with an offline decompose-and-verify judge (Llama-3.3-70B,
4-bit), correct only if fully factual. Cells whose minority class is $<15$ (e.g.\ BioASQ,
near-single-class) are marked \emph{degenerate} and excluded from AUROC ranking. Per-dataset
item and positive counts are in Appendix~\ref{app:stats}.

\paragraph{Baselines.} Beyond verbalized confidence (our $n_1$ rung), we compare against
three established \emph{pure black-box} estimators from the sampling-consistency family,
at their standard $N{=}5$ operating point (3-seed means): \textbf{discrete semantic
entropy} (DSE)~\citep{kuhn2023semantic}, \textbf{lexical similarity} across
samples, and \textbf{EigV}, a graph-Laplacian spectral score over the sample
graph~\citep{lin2024generating}. These are \emph{multi-sample}: they resample the target
answer $N$ times and score the dispersion of the resulting set. They therefore operate in
a different, more expensive regime than CoI-Verbalized, which scores a single fixed answer
and never resamples the target; we report them for context and mark them inapplicable
(``---'') on our single-answer long-form setting, where multi-sample generation was not run.

\paragraph{Protocol.} Generation is cached once per (target, dataset); every estimator
reads the same cached answers (fair comparison). We report \textbf{AUROC} (discrimination
of correct vs.\ incorrect), \textbf{ECE} (calibration), and \textbf{median per-item
latency} measured relative to generator time at batch size~1 with GPU warm-up. Latency is
a first-class metric: a confidence signal that costs more than the answer is not
deployable. For the multi-sample baselines, the reported per-method latency excludes the
$N{-}1$ extra target generations they require upstream.

\subsection{The verification advantage is a minority, and conditional}
Table~\ref{tab:winrate} summarizes the full six-target grid. Discriminative verification
(\nyn/\nsc) beats the best baseline (\nverb/\nphi) on only $2$--$3$ of $11$ datasets on the
8B targets, and---strikingly---on \emph{none} of the $9$ valid cells on the strong open
32B/70B targets (full grid, Appendix~\ref{app:bigopen}), where $\phi$-CoI or verbalized
confidence are already strong ($0.62$--$0.82$).
The blanket claim ``decomposed verification beats verbalized confidence'' is false. Two
regimes buck this: (i) long-form general knowledge, where $\phi$ collapses (below), and
(ii) the closed targets, where verification wins a majority ($4/6$, $3/5$). The intermediate
ablation rungs ($n_2,n_4,n_5$) are dominated by $\{n_1,n_3,n_6,n_7\}$ throughout
(Appendix~\ref{app:ladder}), so we report only the latter here.

\subsection{Self-rated risk collapses on long-form; verification recovers it}
The deterministic-$\phi$ CoI (\nphi), which aggregates self-rated per-claim \emph{risk}, is
not robust to answer length: on LongFact it falls to $0.525$/$0.613$ on the 8B
targets---\emph{below} the plain verbalized floor ($0.540$/$0.623$). A 180-word answer
decomposes into many claims, each assigned a hedged risk near $0.5$; pooling washes out the
signal. Replacing risk with a fresh yes/no decision fixes this: on LongFact, self-consistency
(\nsc) reaches $0.644$/$0.716$, \textbf{$+0.105$/$+0.092$ over verbalized}, holding
decomposition fixed---the gain is the discriminative read, not the chain structure.
Self-consistency ($n_7$) edges decision-framing ($n_6$) where real-but-uncertain knowledge
exists (LongFact $+0.017$/$+0.014$), consistent with sampling instability exposing
hallucination. Multi-sample baselines (semantic entropy, spectral) are \emph{inapplicable} on
long-form---they would resample each 180-word answer $N$ times---so the single-answer verify
rungs are the strongest single-answer signal available there; on short QA, where resampling is
cheap, the spectral baseline EigV ($0.798$ on Llama/TriviaQA) still leads (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}).

\subsection{The advantage is overconfidence, not judge strength}
Table~\ref{tab:medlfqa} is the key result. On MedLFQA, every \emph{open} target---8B and the
strong 70B alike---keeps verbalized confidence above chance ($0.635$--$0.678$), and there
verification \emph{loses}. On the \emph{closed} targets, verbalized confidence is
\emph{inverted}: GPT-4o scores $0.337$ and Claude $0.469$, i.e.\ the model is systematically
\emph{more} confident on its wrong answers. Exactly there, self-consistency verification
recovers a strong signal---GPT-4o $0.337\rightarrow0.675$ ($+0.34$), Claude
$0.469\rightarrow0.593$. Because a strong \emph{open} judge (Llama-70B) fails to benefit while
weaker \emph{closed} judges do, the advantage cannot be attributed to judge capability; it
tracks \textbf{baseline inversion from closed-model overconfidence}. The same pattern recurs
across the closed grid (Table~\ref{tab:closed}): verification wins where verbalized confidence
is near or below chance, and loses where a baseline is already strong (e.g.\ Claude/GSM8K,
verbalized $0.837$).

\subsection{Supporting grids and baselines}
The full 8B suite (Appendix~\ref{app:suite}, Table~\ref{tab:suite}) shows the pattern in
detail: verification wins LongFact, HotpotQA and (Llama) GSM8K, while short-factual, MCQ,
and most health cells go to \nverb\ or \nphi. On the open 8B \emph{and} large targets, MedLFQA
verbalized confidence stays informative and is the best pure black-box signal of any kind---it
beats even the multi-sample baselines (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}: best baseline EigV
$0.62$/$0.53$). Chain inspection explains the open-model behavior: on MedLFQA the 8B per-claim
self-rated risk is uninformative (mean risk $0.385$ for both correct and incorrect answers) and
grounding is a near-constant---so $\phi$ collapses, while a \emph{holistic} verbalized judgment
still separates. What changes on the closed targets is not the chain but the \emph{baseline}:
the closed model's holistic self-rating inverts (overconfident on errors), and the discriminative
per-claim vote, which asks a different question than ``how sure are you,'' is no longer dragged
down with it. The multi-sample baselines (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}) confirm the regime split:
strong on short QA (they resample the target $N{=}5\times$), inapplicable on long-form.

\subsection{Can better aggregation math rescue $\phi$?}
Because $\phi$'s weakness on long-form is an aggregation of per-claim scores, we asked
whether a better aggregator recovers signal. Offline, over the cached CoI features, we
compared the fixed-prior $\phi$ against closed-form pools (mean/max/product of
$1{-}r$; grounding-weighted min/mean) and a cross-validated logistic regression fit on
$13$ CoI features (Appendix~\ref{app:agg}). \textbf{No learned or closed-form aggregator
beats the fixed prior} on any cell: the logistic regression, with more parameters,
\emph{underperforms} $\phi$ everywhere (e.g.\ Llama/TriviaQA $0.689$ vs.\ $0.686$;
Qwen/MedLFQA $0.631$ vs.\ $0.657$), overfitting at $n{\approx}150$. And on MedLFQA every
aggregator sits at $\sim$0.50. The lesson is that the fixed prior is not the bottleneck;
the \emph{inputs} (self-rated risk) are, which is precisely what the verification rungs
replace.

\section{Discussion}
The grid yields a selection rule rather than a universal winner. Discriminative
self-consistency verification ($n_7$) is the right black-box estimator in exactly two
situations: (i) \textbf{long-form} answers, where the deterministic-$\phi$ chain collapses and
verification is the strongest single-answer signal (multi-sample methods being inapplicable);
and (ii) \textbf{overconfident targets}, diagnosable by a verbalized-confidence AUROC near or
below $0.5$, where verification recovers a signal the self-rating has inverted---most sharply on
frontier closed models. Everywhere else---short factual QA, MCQ, and any dataset on a
well-calibrated strong open model---plain verbalized confidence or $\phi$ is as good or better
and far cheaper, and one should not pay for decomposition. The practical implication is a cheap
\emph{router}: measure verbalized confidence's own reliability on held-out data per (target,
domain), and escalate to verification only where that reliability is poor. The headline is not
that decomposition wins, but that \emph{its value is predictable from a one-call diagnostic of
the baseline}, which is what makes it deployable.

\section{Limitations}
Results are single-seed. The large-open (32B/70B) and closed targets are evaluated on the
six datasets with cached generations, not the full twelve, so LongFact and multi-hop are
measured only on 8B---the closed/large columns lack the long-form general cell where 8B
verification wins most, and the ``overconfidence'' account would be strengthened by a
long-form general closed cell. The overconfidence diagnosis is correlational: we observe that
verification wins where verbalized AUROC is low, but do not intervene on calibration directly.
LongFact correctness is judged by a 70B model without retrieval (parametric factuality, not
ground truth); absolute AUROCs inherit that noise, though we rely on the relative ordering.
Verification's calibration (ECE, Appendix~\ref{app:ece}) is worse than verbalized confidence in
raw scale because votes are coarse; discrimination (AUROC) is our headline and post-hoc
calibration is orthogonal. Degenerate near-single-class cells (BioASQ) are excluded.

\section{Conclusion}
Verbalized confidence is a weak but cheap black-box signal, and making its structure explicit
helps only conditionally. Across six targets and up to twelve datasets, replacing self-rated
risk with a discriminative self-consistency verification wins a \emph{minority} of
cells---nothing on strong open 32B/70B models---but wins decisively in two diagnosable regimes:
long-form answers, where the deterministic chain collapses, and overconfident targets, where
verbalized confidence inverts (below chance) and verification recovers it, most sharply on
frontier closed models ($0.34\rightarrow0.68$ on GPT-4o health long-form). The advantage tracks
\emph{baseline inversion, not judge capability}---a strong open judge still loses where a weaker
overconfident closed judge wins---so a one-call reliability check on verbalized confidence
predicts when to escalate. We release the estimators and evaluation harness.

\section{Aggregation search}\label{app:agg}

pools ($1{-}$mean, $1{-}$max, $\prod(1{-}r)$, noisy-OR), grounding-weighted mean/min pools of
$\mathrm{base}\cdot(1{-}r)$, and a cross-validated logistic regression over $13$ CoI features.
