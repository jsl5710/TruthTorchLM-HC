\begin{abstract}
Verbalized confidence---asking a model to rate how likely its own answer is correct---is the
default black-box uncertainty signal, but it is overconfident and poorly calibrated on
long-form answers. A natural fix is to decompose the answer into atomic claims and verify each
one; we ask when that actually pays. An ablation ladder separates a deterministic aggregation
($\phi$) of self-rated per-claim \emph{risk} from a \emph{discriminative} verification
step---a fresh yes/no ``Is this claim correct?'' asked once or $K$ times (self-consistency).
Across six targets (two 8B open, two 32B/70B open, and closed GPT-4o and Claude-Haiku) and up
to twelve datasets, excluding cells too class-imbalanced to support an AUROC, the result is
mostly \emph{negative}: verification wins a minority of cells---$2$--$3$ of $11$ on 8B targets
and \textbf{none of the $9$} on the strong open 32B/70B targets---and only three wins in the
grid exceed a $\pm0.04$ noise band. The exception is robust and replicated: on \textbf{long-form
general-knowledge factuality} (LongFact, $n{=}300$), self-consistency verification gains
$+0.104$ and $+0.093$ AUROC over verbalized confidence on both 8B targets, in the regime where
multi-sample baselines cannot be applied at all; there $\phi$ is no better than the verbalized
floor. We also test whether verification rescues \emph{overconfident} targets whose verbalized
AUROC falls below chance: of the two inverted cells with adequate class balance, it recovers
one and fails the other, so we report this as an open question, not a mechanism. The
recommendation is conditional---verify long-form answers, otherwise keep the single verbalized
call, which is $40$--$70\times$ cheaper.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Deployed systems increasingly consume answers from language models they cannot inspect:
no logits, no hidden states, no weights, only text. The cheapest confidence signal in
this setting is \emph{verbalized confidence}~\citep{tian2023just,kadavath2022language}:
prompt the model to rate, on a $0$--$100$ scale, how likely its answer is correct. It is
one extra call and needs no training, but it is systematically overconfident, and on
long-form answers---where correctness is a property of many interacting facts---a single
global self-rating is a coarse summary of a structured object.

A natural fix is to make the structure explicit. \textbf{Chain-of-Interaction (CoI)}
verbalized confidence judges a fixed candidate answer $y$ for a query $x$ by (i)
decomposing $y$ into atomic, independently checkable claims, (ii) gathering evidence and
an adversarial critique per claim, and (iii) scanning for cross-claim contradictions;
the confidence \emph{score} is then computed deterministically in code (a fixed-prior
aggregation $\phi$) rather than self-rated, which removes the same-pass overconfidence of
plain verbalized confidence. This is attractive in principle, but raises an empirical
question we answer here: \emph{which part of the chain actually carries the signal?}

We study an ablation ladder (\S\ref{sec:method}) over a six-target grid and report four
findings (\S\ref{sec:results}).
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{The verification advantage is a minority, not a majority.} Across the full
suite it beats the best baseline on only $2$--$3$ of $11$ datasets on 8B targets and
\textbf{$0$ of $9$ on the strong open 32B/70B targets}, where $\phi$ or verbalized
confidence is already strong. The blanket claim ``decomposition beats verbalized
confidence'' is false, and we report this negative result as our primary finding.
\item \textbf{The one robust gain is long-form general knowledge.} On LongFact
($n{=}300$ per target, the largest cells in the study) self-consistency verification
improves AUROC by $+0.104$ and $+0.093$ over verbalized confidence, replicated on two
independent 8B targets and $2.5\times$ the $\pm0.04$ noise band. It is also the one regime
where multi-sample baselines are inapplicable, since they would resample a 180-word answer.
\item \textbf{Self-rated risk is the weak link, but ``collapse'' is overstated.} The
deterministic-$\phi$ CoI is competitive on some short QA and is \emph{no better than the
verbalized floor} on LongFact ($0.525$/$0.613$ vs.\ $0.540$/$0.623$, within noise). What
recovers signal there is replacing self-rated risk with a discriminative yes/no verification,
holding decomposition fixed.
\item \textbf{Whether verification rescues overconfident targets is unresolved.} We test the
natural hypothesis that it helps where verbalized AUROC is \emph{inverted} (below chance).
Only two inverted cells survive our class-balance criterion, and they disagree: verification
recovers one (Claude/TruthfulQA, $0.48\rightarrow0.60$) and fails the other
(GPT-4o/BioASQ, $0.35$, where $\phi$ recovers to $0.62$ but verification reaches only $0.47$).
We report this as an open question, not a mechanism (\S\ref{sec:inversion}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Method: CoI-Verbalized}\label{sec:method}
All estimators take a query $x$ and a \emph{fixed} candidate answer $y$ (never rewriting
$y$) and return a confidence in $[0,1]$; higher means more likely correct. They are
\emph{pure black-box}: they read only text, and the judge model is the target itself. We
organize them as a ladder of ``interaction chains'' $n$.

\paragraph{$n_1$: Verbalized confidence (floor).} One call: ``Judge how likely $y$ is
correct; return \{confidence: 0--100\}.'' The standard baseline.

\paragraph{$n_3$: Deterministic $\phi$ (CoI default).} Decompose $y$ into claims; per
claim, the model returns a \emph{grounding} label (contextual/parametric/inferred), an
evidence sentence, a counter-argument, and a self-rated \emph{risk} $r\in[0,1]$; a
consistency chain lists contradictions. Confidence is computed in code:
each claim scores $s = \mathrm{base}[\text{grounding}]\cdot(1-r)\cdot\text{penalty}$,
with a fixed prior $\mathrm{base}=\{1.0,0.85,0.65\}$ and a contradiction penalty; claims
are pooled by a min/mean rule ($\phi$). No training data is used.

\paragraph{$n_6$: Decision-framed verification.} Decompose $y$ into claims; then, instead
of a self-rated risk, issue a \emph{fresh} discriminative call per claim: ``Is the
statement factually correct and a correct answer to the question? Answer YES or NO.''
The per-claim confidence is $1$ for YES and $0$ for NO; claims are pooled by the same
min/mean rule. One verification call per claim (greedy).

\paragraph{$n_7$: Self-consistency verification.} As $n_6$, but each claim is verified
$K{=}5$ times at temperature $0.7$; the per-claim confidence is the \emph{YES-vote
fraction}. The intuition is that hallucinated facts are unstable across samples while
known facts are stable, so vote entropy discriminates where a single self-rating cannot.

The ladder isolates two design choices: $n_1{\to}n_3$ swaps a global self-rating for a
structured self-rating; $n_3{\to}n_6$ swaps self-rated \emph{risk} for a discriminative
\emph{decision}; $n_6{\to}n_7$ adds sampling-based consistency. All chains run with model
``thinking'' disabled, so latency reflects the estimator, not incidental reasoning.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}
\paragraph{Targets.} Six models spanning the capability and access spectrum, each used as
both generator and its own chain-judge (pure black-box): two \textbf{8B open}
(Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen3-8B), two \textbf{large open} (Qwen3-32B, Llama-3.3-70B,
4-bit), and two \textbf{frontier closed} accessed via a gateway (GPT-4o, Claude-Haiku-4.5).
The spread lets us separate judge \emph{capability} from judge \emph{calibration}.

\paragraph{Datasets.} Up to twelve, spanning format (short-factual, long-form, MCQ,
multi-hop, adversarial) and domain (general, health, math): TriviaQA, NaturalQA, PopQA
(general short), \textbf{LongFact}~\citep{wei2024longfact} (general long-form, 300 prompts),
TruthfulQA (adversarial), MedQA/MMLU-Med (MCQ), BioASQ/K-QA (health free), MedLFQA (health
long-form), GSM8K (math), HotpotQA (multi-hop). Each is 150 items unless noted; closed and
large-open targets are evaluated on the subset with cached generations (six datasets each).
Correctness is a gold-grounded LLM judge (Qwen3-4B; letter-match for MCQ); LongFact has no
gold, so we label factuality with an offline decompose-and-verify judge (Llama-3.3-70B,
4-bit), correct only if fully factual. Cells whose minority class is $<15$ (e.g.\ BioASQ,
near-single-class) are marked \emph{degenerate} and excluded from AUROC ranking. Per-dataset
item and positive counts are in Appendix~\ref{app:stats}.

\paragraph{Baselines.} Beyond verbalized confidence (our $n_1$ rung), we compare against
three established \emph{pure black-box} estimators from the sampling-consistency family,
at their standard $N{=}5$ operating point (3-seed means): \textbf{discrete semantic
entropy} (DSE)~\citep{kuhn2023semantic}, \textbf{lexical similarity} across
samples, and \textbf{EigV}, a graph-Laplacian spectral score over the sample
graph~\citep{lin2024generating}. These are \emph{multi-sample}: they resample the target
answer $N$ times and score the dispersion of the resulting set. They therefore operate in
a different, more expensive regime than CoI-Verbalized, which scores a single fixed answer
and never resamples the target; we report them for context and mark them inapplicable
(``---'') on our single-answer long-form setting, where multi-sample generation was not run.

\paragraph{Protocol.} Generation is cached once per (target, dataset); every estimator
reads the same cached answers (fair comparison). We report \textbf{AUROC} (discrimination
of correct vs.\ incorrect), \textbf{ECE} (calibration), and \textbf{median per-item
latency} measured relative to generator time at batch size~1 with GPU warm-up. Latency is
a first-class metric: a confidence signal that costs more than the answer is not
deployable. For the multi-sample baselines, the reported per-method latency excludes the
$N{-}1$ extra target generations they require upstream.

\subsection{The verification advantage is a minority, and conditional}
Table~\ref{tab:winrate} summarizes the full six-target grid. Discriminative verification
(\nyn/\nsc) beats the best baseline (\nverb/\nphi) on only $2$--$3$ of $11$ datasets on the
8B targets, and---strikingly---on \emph{none} of the $9$ valid cells on the strong open
32B/70B targets (full grid, Appendix~\ref{app:bigopen}), where $\phi$-CoI or verbalized
confidence are already strong ($0.62$--$0.82$).
The blanket claim ``decomposed verification beats verbalized confidence'' is false. Moreover,
only three wins in the entire grid exceed the $\pm0.04$ noise band at $n{\approx}150$: the two
LongFact cells and Claude/TruthfulQA. The intermediate ablation rungs ($n_2,n_4,n_5$) are
dominated by $\{n_1,n_3,n_6,n_7\}$ throughout (Appendix~\ref{app:ladder}), so we report only
the latter here.

\subsection{The robust exception: long-form general knowledge}
Table~\ref{tab:longfact} is the paper's one replicated, above-noise result. On LongFact,
self-consistency verification (\nsc) reaches $0.644$/$0.716$ against a verbalized floor of
$0.540$/$0.623$---\textbf{$+0.104$ and $+0.093$}---on two independent 8B targets, at $n{=}300$
with 61 and 41 negatives (the largest and best-balanced cells in the study). The gain is
attributable to the \emph{discriminative read} rather than to decomposition, because the
deterministic-$\phi$ rung shares the identical decomposition and does not benefit: $\phi$
scores $0.525$/$0.613$, statistically indistinguishable from the verbalized floor. We
therefore avoid the word ``collapse'' for $\phi$ on long-form; the honest statement is that
structured self-rated risk buys nothing here, while a fresh yes/no verification buys $\sim0.10$.
Self-consistency ($n_7$) edges decision-framing ($n_6$) by $+0.017$/$+0.014$, which is inside
noise; we do not claim a difference between them, and note that $n_7$ is also the less stable
rung elsewhere (e.g.\ $0.378$ on GPT-4o/GSM8K). Multi-sample baselines are \emph{inapplicable}
in this regime---they would resample each 180-word answer $N$ times---so verification is the
strongest single-answer signal available for long-form; on short QA, where resampling is cheap,
the spectral baseline EigV ($0.798$ on Llama/TriviaQA) still leads (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}).

\subsection{Does verification rescue an inverted baseline?}\label{sec:inversion}
A natural hypothesis for \emph{when} verification helps is that it rescues targets whose own
verbalized confidence is \emph{inverted}---AUROC below chance, i.e.\ the model is more
confident on its wrong answers. Table~\ref{tab:inversion} collects every inverted cell in the
grid. The evidence does not settle it. Two inverted cells survive our class-balance criterion
and they point in opposite directions: on Claude/TruthfulQA verification recovers the signal
($0.484\rightarrow0.596$), but on GPT-4o/BioASQ---the most strongly inverted valid cell
($0.346$)---verification reaches only $0.466$ while the deterministic $\phi$ recovers to
$0.622$. The two cells that would have supported a clean story, GPT-4o and Claude on MedLFQA,
have 7 and 8 negatives respectively and are excluded under the criterion in
\S\ref{sec:setup}; we show them in Table~\ref{tab:inversion} only for transparency and base no
claim on them. We also note a design confound that this grid cannot break: every inverted
target here is closed and every calibrated target is open, so ``inversion'' and
``closed-weights'' are perfectly collinear. Establishing the hypothesis would require inverted
\emph{open} targets (or calibrated closed ones) and long-form cells with adequate negatives on
the closed arm---neither of which our cached generations provide.

\subsection{Supporting grids and baselines}
The full 8B suite (Appendix~\ref{app:suite}, Table~\ref{tab:suite}) shows the pattern in
detail: verification wins LongFact, HotpotQA and (Llama) GSM8K, while short-factual, MCQ,
and most health cells go to \nverb\ or \nphi. On the open 8B \emph{and} large targets, MedLFQA
verbalized confidence stays informative and is the best pure black-box signal of any kind---it
beats even the multi-sample baselines (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}: best baseline EigV
$0.62$/$0.53$). Chain inspection explains the open-model behavior: on MedLFQA the 8B per-claim
self-rated risk is uninformative (mean risk $0.385$ for both correct and incorrect answers) and
grounding is a near-constant---so $\phi$ collapses, while a \emph{holistic} verbalized judgment
still separates. What changes on the closed targets is not the chain but the \emph{baseline}:
the closed model's holistic self-rating inverts (overconfident on errors), and the discriminative
per-claim vote, which asks a different question than ``how sure are you,'' is no longer dragged
down with it. The multi-sample baselines (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}) confirm the regime split:
strong on short QA (they resample the target $N{=}5\times$), inapplicable on long-form.

\subsection{Can better aggregation math rescue $\phi$?}
Because $\phi$'s weakness on long-form is an aggregation of per-claim scores, we asked
whether a better aggregator recovers signal. Offline, over the cached CoI features, we
compared the fixed-prior $\phi$ against closed-form pools (mean/max/product of
$1{-}r$; grounding-weighted min/mean) and a cross-validated logistic regression fit on
$13$ CoI features (Appendix~\ref{app:agg}). \textbf{No aggregator improves on the fixed prior
by more than the $\pm0.04$ noise band.} Differences run in both directions and are all small:
a min-pool of $\mathrm{base}\cdot(1{-}r)$ is nominally ahead on Llama/TriviaQA ($0.711$ vs.\
$\phi$'s $0.686$) while the cross-validated logistic regression is nominally behind on
Qwen/MedLFQA ($0.631$ vs.\ $0.657$); none of these gaps is resolvable at $n{\approx}150$.
Notably the learned aggregator, despite $13$ features, never pulls clear of a fixed prior with
no training data at all. And on MedLFQA every aggregator sits near $0.50$. The lesson is that
the aggregator is not the bottleneck; the \emph{inputs} (self-rated risk) are, which is
precisely what the verification rungs replace.

\section{Discussion}
The grid yields a narrow recommendation rather than a universal winner. Decomposed
verification earns its cost in one demonstrated setting: \textbf{long-form answers}, where it
gains $\sim0.10$ AUROC over verbalized confidence on both targets we could measure, and where
multi-sample methods are inapplicable because they would resample the whole answer. Everywhere
else in our grid---short factual QA, MCQ, math, and \emph{every} dataset on a strong open
32B/70B target---plain verbalized confidence or $\phi$ is as good or better and $40$--$70\times$
cheaper, so decomposition should not be paid for by default.

We had expected a second regime, \textbf{overconfident targets}, where a below-chance verbalized
AUROC signals that the self-rating is anti-correlated with correctness. Our data cannot support
that claim (\S\ref{sec:inversion}), and we flag two reasons it remains attractive but unproven.
First, the obvious control is missing from our comparison: if verbalized confidence is
\emph{reliably} inverted, simply negating it is a one-call fix, and on the cells where
verification wins it does so by a margin over negated-verbalized that is far smaller than the
margin over verbalized itself. Second, ``inverted'' and ``closed-weights'' are perfectly
confounded in this grid. A deployable router of the form ``measure verbalized reliability on
held-out data, escalate where it is poor'' is therefore a hypothesis our results motivate but do
not establish; testing it needs inverted open targets and long-form closed cells with adequate
class balance.

\section{Conclusion}
Verbalized confidence is a weak but very cheap black-box signal, and making its structure
explicit helps only conditionally. Across six targets and up to twelve datasets, replacing
self-rated risk with a discriminative verification wins a \emph{minority} of cells---$2$--$3$ of
$11$ on 8B targets and none of the $9$ valid cells on strong open 32B/70B models---and only
three wins in the entire grid exceed the noise band. The exception is robust and replicated:
on long-form general-knowledge factuality, self-consistency verification gains $+0.104$ and
$+0.093$ AUROC over verbalized confidence on two independent targets, in the largest and
best-balanced cells we measure, and it is the regime where multi-sample baselines cannot be
applied at all. The tempting further story---that verification rescues overconfident targets
whose verbalized signal has inverted---is not established by our data: of the two inverted cells
with adequate class balance, verification recovers one and fails the other, and inversion is
confounded with closed-weights throughout our grid. We therefore offer a conditional
recommendation (verify long-form, otherwise keep the single call) and release the estimators,
the harness, and the per-cell class balances needed to check these claims.

\section*{Limitations}
\paragraph{Statistical power.} Every CoI number is \emph{single-seed}, while the multi-sample
baselines we quote are 3-seed means---so Table~\ref{tab:baselines} is not a symmetric
comparison. The 8B cells use seed 1 and the large-open and closed cells seed 0, so the two
halves of the grid do not even share a seed. We have no seed variance, and at $n{\approx}150$
the AUROC standard error is roughly $\pm0.045$; we therefore treat $\pm0.04$ as a noise band,
decline to rank differences inside it, and rest the paper's positive claim on the LongFact cells
($n{=}300$), which are the only ones with a comfortable margin. Per-cell bootstrap intervals
would sharpen every comparison here and are the obvious next step.

\paragraph{Class balance and excluded cells.} We exclude cells whose minority class is $<15$.
This removes BioASQ on all open targets (as few as one negative) and both closed MedLFQA and
GSM8K cells (4--8 negatives). Those closed cells are exactly where a clean
``verification rescues overconfidence'' story would have lived, so its absence is a limitation
of the data, not evidence against the hypothesis. Per-cell counts are in
Appendix~\ref{app:stats} for the 8B targets and in Table~\ref{tab:closed} for the closed arm.

\paragraph{Coverage and confounds.} The large-open and closed targets are evaluated on the six
datasets with cached generations, not the full twelve, so LongFact and multi-hop are measured
only on 8B. The long-form and overconfidence conditions therefore never co-occur in a single
non-degenerate cell. Moreover every inverted target in our grid is closed and every calibrated
one is open, so inversion and closed-weights are collinear and we cannot attribute the effect to
either. We also do not compare against the obvious cheap control for an inverted baseline,
\emph{negated} verbalized confidence, which would cost one call rather than $K$; adding it is
necessary before recommending verification on inversion grounds.

\paragraph{Labels.} Correctness comes from an LLM judge (Qwen3-4B; letter-match for MCQ) that we
did not validate against human annotation, and LongFact factuality from a 70B judge without
retrieval---parametric factuality, not ground truth. Absolute AUROCs inherit that noise; we rely
on relative orderings within a cell, where judge error is at least shared across estimators.

\paragraph{Calibration.} Verification's ECE (Appendix~\ref{app:ece}) is worse than verbalized
confidence in raw scale because votes are coarse. Discrimination (AUROC) is our headline and
post-hoc calibration is orthogonal, but a deployed router would need calibrated scores.

\section*{Ethics Statement}
Four of our datasets are medical (MedQA, MMLU-Med, BioASQ, K-QA, MedLFQA) and the deployment
story we discuss---gating or escalating answers by a confidence score---would, in a health
setting, decide which model outputs reach a person. We report that no estimator we tested is
reliable on health long-form: the best AUROC is around $0.65$, and on several health cells every
method is near chance. A confidence signal at that level must not be used to suppress review of
medical content, and we explicitly do not recommend deploying these estimators as a safety gate
in clinical or patient-facing settings. All datasets are public and used under their licenses;
no human-subjects data was collected and no annotation was crowdsourced for this work.
